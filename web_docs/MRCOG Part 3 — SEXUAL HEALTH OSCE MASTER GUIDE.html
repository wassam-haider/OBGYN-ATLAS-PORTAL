MRCOG Part 3 — SEXUAL HEALTH OSCE MASTER GUIDE

⭐ For MRCOG Part 3, sexual-health stations are rarely just “name the diagnosis.” The examiner is assessing whether you can take a sensitive sexual history, examine appropriately, interpret bedside tests, diagnose safely, treat/arrange follow-up, partner-notify, prevent transmission, address fertility/pregnancy implications, and recognise safeguarding concerns.

SECTION 1

Overview & Core Guidance

For STI-specific management, the BASHH guidelines are particularly important alongside RCOG/NICE, and BASHH currently lists updated guidance for anogenital herpes (2024), anogenital warts (2024), chlamydia (2026), and other sexual-health categories.

1. UNIVERSAL SEXUAL-HEALTH OSCE APPROACH

A

Opening the consultation

Introduction

Wash hands.

Introduce yourself.

Confirm patient identity.

Confirm age.

Explain confidentiality.

Establish whether they are comfortable discussing sexual health.

Offer a chaperone for examination.

Obtain consent before examination/swabs.

Useful opening

“I understand you have some concerns regarding your sexual or genital health. I ask these questions routinely because they help me provide the correct care. Everything you tell me is confidential, subject to the usual safeguarding exceptions. Is it okay if I ask you some personal questions?”

Confidentiality caveat

Explain:

Information is confidential.

Exceptions include:

serious risk of harm to patient/another person

safeguarding concerns

certain legal/public-health requirements.

➔ Candida → itch predominates.

Usually:

<4.5

SECTION 5

5. BACTERIAL VAGINOSIS

Cause

Disruption of normal Lactobacillus-dominant flora with increased anaerobic organisms.

Common organisms include:

Gardnerella vaginalis

anaerobes

Atopobium and others.

Symptoms

Often asymptomatic.

Typical:

Thin homogeneous discharge.

Grey/white discharge.

Fishy odour.

Odour particularly noticeable after intercourse/menstruation.

Usually minimal inflammation.

pH

>4.5

Amsel criteria

Four criteria:

Homogeneous thin discharge.

Vaginal pH >4.5.

Positive whiff test.

Clue cells on microscopy.

Diagnosis

≥3 of 4 = BV by Amsel criteria.

Clue cells

Vaginal epithelial cells coated with bacteria.

Microscopically:

stippled appearance

poorly defined cell borders.

Whiff test

Add KOH to vaginal discharge.

Positive:

fishy/amines odour

Nugent score

Gram-stain scoring:

0–3: normal flora

4–6: intermediate

7–10: BV

This is a very useful numerical value for MRCOG.

Organism

Sexually transmitted protozoan.

Symptoms

Frothy discharge.

Yellow-green discharge.

Offensive odour.

Vulval irritation.

Dysuria.

Dyspareunia.

Can be asymptomatic.

Examination

Classic

“Strawberry cervix”

punctate cervical haemorrhages.

erythematous cervix.

pH

Usually:

>4.5

Microscopy

Wet mount:

motile flagellated organisms.

However, wet microscopy has limited sensitivity.

Preferred test

NAAT is much more sensitive.

Important OSCE point

Treat:

Patient + sexual partner(s)

because reinfection is common.

8. CERVICITIS

Definition

Inflammation of the cervix.

Common causes

Infectious

Chlamydia trachomatis.

Neisseria gonorrhoeae.

Mycoplasma genitalium.

Trichomonas vaginalis.

HSV can occasionally be associated.

Non-infectious

Chemical irritation.

Foreign body.

Cervical ectropion.

Trauma.

Instrumentation.

Clinical features

Abnormal vaginal discharge.

Mucopurulent discharge.

Postcoital bleeding.

Intermenstrual bleeding.

Dyspareunia.

Dysuria.

Pelvic pain may indicate ascending infection/PID.

Examination

Speculum

Look for:

mucopurulent discharge from cervical os.

cervical erythema.

friability.

contact bleeding.

ectropion.

ulcers.

masses.

Key finding

Cervical motion/contact may cause bleeding.

Investigations

NAAT

Test for:

Chlamydia.

Gonorrhoea.

Depending on circumstances:

Trichomonas.

M

genitalium.

Also consider:

HIV.

Syphilis.

Hepatitis B/C depending on risk.

Treatment principle

Treat according to:

organism.

local resistance.

pregnancy status.

allergy.

STI risk.

current BASHH/local guideline.

For example, CDC guidance lists doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days as a cervicitis regimen, with gonorrhoea treatment added when indicated; local/BASHH guidance should be followed in UK-style MRCOG answers.

Pregnancy

Do not automatically use doxycycline.

Choose a pregnancy-appropriate regimen according to current guideline.

SECTION 9

9. PID — HIGH-YIELD MRCOG STATION

Definition

Ascending infection/inflammation involving:

➔ endometrium → endometritis
➔ fallopian tubes → salpingitis
➔ ovaries/tubes → tubo-ovarian abscess
➔ pelvic peritoneum → pelvic peritonitis.

PID can be caused by STI organisms as well as vaginal flora/anaerobes.

Risk factors

Young age.

New sexual partner.

Multiple sexual partners.

Previous STI.

Previous PID.

Previous chlamydia/gonorrhoea.

Recent uterine instrumentation.

Recent insertion of an IUD — particularly around insertion period.

Symptoms

Classical triad

Lower abdominal/pelvic pain.

Abnormal vaginal discharge.

Abnormal bleeding.

Other:

Deep dyspareunia.

Postcoital bleeding.

Intermenstrual bleeding.

Fever.

Dysuria.

Nausea/vomiting.

PID can be mild or asymptomatic.

Examination

Abdominal

suprapubic tenderness

lower abdominal tenderness

guarding/peritonism if severe.

Pelvic

The 3 minimum clinical criteria:

Cervical motion tenderness.

Uterine tenderness.

Adnexal tenderness.

If a sexually active woman at STI risk has pelvic/lower abdominal pain with no better explanation and ≥1 of these three, empiric PID treatment should be considered.

PID red flags

Urgent assessment/admission if:

severe illness.

sepsis.

severe abdominal pain.

peritonism.

tubo-ovarian abscess.

pregnancy.

surgical emergency cannot be excluded.

unable to tolerate oral treatment.

failure to improve on outpatient treatment.

diagnostic uncertainty.

PID differential

Always consider:

Ectopic pregnancy.

Appendicitis.

Ovarian torsion.

Ruptured ovarian cyst.

Endometriosis.

UTI/pyelonephritis.

Gastrointestinal disease.

MRCOG golden rule

Always exclude pregnancy/ectopic pregnancy in reproductive-age women with pelvic pain.

PID investigations

Immediate

Pregnancy test.

Urinalysis.

FBC.

CRP.

U&E/LFT where appropriate.

STI NAAT.

HIV/syphilis ± hepatitis testing depending on risk.

Imaging

Pelvic ultrasound

Look for:

tubo-ovarian abscess.

hydrosalpinx.

pyosalpinx.

free fluid.

Long-term PID complications

Extremely important for counselling:

Tubal factor infertility.

Ectopic pregnancy.

Chronic pelvic pain.

Recurrent PID.

Tubo-ovarian abscess.

Therefore:

Do not delay treatment while waiting for all test results when clinical PID is suspected.

Early treatment matters because even mild/unrecognised PID can be associated with reproductive complications.

SECTION 12

12. GENITAL HERPES

Cause

HSV-1 or HSV-2.

HSV-2 traditionally causes more recurrent genital disease, although genital HSV-1 is increasingly recognised.

Primary episode

May cause:

painful grouped vesicles.

painful ulcers.

dysuria.

vaginal discharge.

tender inguinal lymph nodes.

fever.

malaise.

headache.

Severe primary disease can cause urinary retention or neurological complications.

Examination

Look for:

vesicles.

shallow ulcers.

crusting.

erythema.

tender inguinal nodes.

Diagnosis

Preferred

Lesion swab for:

HSV PCR/NAAT

Type HSV-1 versus HSV-2 when possible.

Important counselling

HSV:

establishes lifelong latency.

can recur.

can be transmitted during asymptomatic shedding.

may be transmitted by people without obvious lesions.

CDC specifically emphasises asymptomatic shedding and transmission counselling.

First episode treatment

Example CDC regimen:

Acyclovir 400 mg PO three times daily for 7–10 days

or

Valaciclovir 1 g PO twice daily for 7–10 days

Treatment may be extended if lesions have not healed by 10 days.

For MRCOG:

“I would follow the current BASHH regimen.”

Do not rely on an old memorised regimen if the station asks for current UK guidance.

Recurrent herpes

Options:

episodic treatment.

suppressive therapy for frequent/severe recurrences.

suppressive treatment can also be considered when psychological distress is substantial.

Pregnancy

This is an important MRCOG crossover.

Ask:

first episode or recurrent?

gestation?

lesions/prodrome at labour?

previous history?

treatment.

Management is obstetric-led and depends on timing, recurrence, lesions/prodrome and delivery circumstances.

13. SYPHILIS

Organism

Treponema pallidum

Primary syphilis

Classic lesion

Chancre

usually single.

painless.

clean-based.

indurated.

regional lymphadenopathy.

Secondary syphilis

Features:

widespread rash.

palms and soles.

mucous patches.

condyloma lata.

lymphadenopathy.

systemic symptoms.

Important differential

Condyloma lata ≠ genital wart.

Condyloma lata occurs in secondary syphilis.

Tertiary syphilis

May cause:

cardiovascular disease.

neurological disease.

gummatous disease.

Investigations

Serology:

treponemal tests.

non-treponemal tests such as RPR/VDRL.

Important numerical concept

RPR/VDRL titres are useful for:

baseline.

monitoring treatment response.

detecting reinfection.

A clinically significant change is generally a fourfold change in titre, equivalent to a two-dilution change.

SECTION 14

14. GENITAL WARTS

Cause

HPV.

Approximately 90% of anogenital warts are associated with HPV 6 and 11, which are low-oncogenic-risk types.

Appearance
papular.

flat.

pedunculated.

cauliflower-like.

flesh-coloured.

Sites:

vulva.

vagina.

cervix.

perineum.

perianal region.

anus.

Diagnosis

Usually:

Clinical examination.

HPV testing is not recommended simply to diagnose genital warts.

When to biopsy

Biopsy if:

pigmented.

indurated.

fixed.

ulcerated.

bleeding.

atypical.

diagnosis uncertain.

immunocompromised patient.

failure to respond to treatment.

lesion worsens.

Treatment options

Patient-applied

Imiquimod.

Podophyllotoxin/podofilox where appropriate.

Clinician-applied

Cryotherapy.

TCA/BCA.

Surgical excision.

Electrosurgery.

Laser in selected cases.

Treatment depends on:

number.

size.

location.

pregnancy.

patient preference.

cost.

clinician expertise.

Pregnancy

Very important:

Avoid podophyllin/podophyllotoxin and sinecatechins during pregnancy.

Imiquimod is generally avoided until better pregnancy safety data are available.

Cryotherapy or appropriate surgical treatment may be considered if required.

HPV COUNSELLING

Explain:

HPV is extremely common.

Infection may be asymptomatic.

Warts can appear months or years after acquisition.

Therefore, appearance of warts does not establish when HPV was acquired or prove recent infidelity.

Treatment removes visible warts but does not necessarily eradicate HPV.

Recurrence can occur.

HPV vaccination prevents infection with vaccine-covered types but does not treat existing infection/warts.
