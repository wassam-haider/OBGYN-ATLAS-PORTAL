SECTION 1

Overview & Core Guidance

➔ Recognise → focused history → examination → investigations → differential → management → safety-net → shared decision-making.

The current RCOG curriculum specifically expects knowledge of menopause/HRT, POI, DEXA indications, amenorrhoea and its causes, and interpretation of amenorrhoea investigations.

Important
Numerical thresholds below are useful examination figures, but local protocols can differ. In the MRCOG station, always state that you will follow local/NICE/RCOG guidance where appropriate.
SECTION 2

1. MENOPAUSE

Definition
Natural menopause

Permanent cessation of menstruation due to loss of ovarian follicular activity.

Retrospectively diagnosed after 12 consecutive months of amenorrhoea with no other cause.

Typical age:

Most commonly 45–55 years

UK average ≈ 51 years

RCOG notes the average may be lower in some Asian populations, including Pakistan/India.

Perimenopause

Transition preceding menopause.

May have:

Cycle irregularity

Hot flushes

Night sweats

Sleep disturbance

Mood symptoms

Vaginal/sexual symptoms.

Premature ovarian insufficiency — POI

Loss of ovarian function before 40 years.

Important distinction
Early menopause: 40–44 years

POI: <40 years.

SECTION 5

4. MENOPAUSE — INVESTIGATIONS

Woman ≥45 with typical symptoms

Usually:

No routine FSH needed.

Diagnosis is generally clinical.

RCOG/NICE state that women over 45 with typical menopausal symptoms usually do not require hormone testing.

Consider FSH when:

Age 40–45 with menopausal symptoms

Age <40 with suspected POI

Diagnosis is uncertain.

FSH

Typical ovarian failure pattern:

↑ FSH

➔ ↓ estradiol

Do not diagnose POI from a single borderline result alone.

SECTION 7

6. HRT — THE CORE MRCOG STATION

The examiner may give:

“A 52-year-old woman with hot flushes asks whether she should start HRT.”

Your framework:

1

Confirm indication

HRT is particularly effective for:

Vasomotor symptoms

Menopausal symptoms

GSM

Prevention of bone loss in appropriate women.

2

Determine uterine status

Has she got a uterus?

Uterus HRT
Uterus present Oestrogen + progestogen
Total hysterectomy Usually oestrogen-only
Hysterectomy + significant endometriosis history Consider combined therapy
LNG-IUS Can provide endometrial protection with systemic oestrogen

NICE recommends combined HRT for women with a uterus and oestrogen-only HRT after total hysterectomy.

SECTION 8

7. CYCLICAL VS CONTINUOUS HRT

Sequential/cyclical HRT

Usually appropriate when:

Perimenopausal

Still having periods

Within approximately 12 months of last menstrual period.

Expected:

Withdrawal bleed.

Continuous combined HRT

Usually for:

Postmenopausal woman

≥12 months since last period.

Goal:

Amenorrhoea/no scheduled bleeding.

RCOG describes cyclical treatment for women who start combined HRT before menopause or within 12 months of the last period, and continuous combined HRT when started >12 months after the last period.

SECTION 9

8. HRT — ROUTE

Oral oestrogen

Advantages:

Convenient

Cheap

Effective.

Disadvantages:

Increased VTE risk compared with transdermal route.

First-pass hepatic effect.

Transdermal

Patch/gel/spray.

Advantages:

Avoids first-pass hepatic metabolism.

Preferred when VTE risk is increased.

Important OSCE point

Obesity is a VTE risk factor; consider transdermal rather than oral oestrogen.

RCOG specifically notes that women with VTE risk factors such as BMI ≥30 should generally be offered transdermal oestrogen.

SECTION 11

10. HRT RISKS — EXAM TABLE

Issue Important point
Breast cancer Risk varies with regimen/duration
VTE Greater with oral than transdermal oestrogen
Stroke Small increased risk particularly with oral therapy
Endometrium Unopposed systemic oestrogen increases hyperplasia/cancer risk
Gallbladder Risk may increase
Cardiovascular Benefit-risk depends strongly on age/timing/baseline risk

RCOG notes that combined HRT can increase breast cancer risk, while oestrogen-only therapy has little or no increased breast cancer risk in the evidence summarised in its patient guidance.

SECTION 28

27. ADOLESCENT MENSTRUAL NUMBERS

Typical adolescent cycle:

21–45 days

Bleeding usually 2–7 days.

Bleeding needing product changes every 1–2 hours, especially >7 days, suggests heavy menstrual bleeding.

An adolescent should be assessed for amenorrhoea if:

≥15 years and no menarche, OR

≥3 years after breast development without menarche.

Also:

>90 days between periods is not considered normal even in adolescence and warrants evaluation.

SECTION 43

43. FEMALE GENITAL MUTILATION — FGM

RCOG definition

Partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to female genital organs for non-medical reasons.

It has:

No health benefit.

It can cause physical, psychological and psychosexual harm.

➔ → Provide compassionate medical care.

Child with FGM

➔ → Safeguarding issue.

Child at risk of FGM

➔ → Safeguarding assessment and appropriate protective action.

RCOG states that children with FGM or suspected FGM should be seen within child safeguarding services.

Do not:

Blame family

Shame patient

Make assumptions about culture

Assume parents are perpetrators

Promise absolute confidentiality where safeguarding concerns exist.

SECTION 53

55. HIGH-YIELD MRCOG STATION CLOSING

Whatever the station, finish with:

“I would explain my findings and differential diagnosis to the patient, discuss the appropriate investigations and management options, involve her in shared decision-making, address psychological and sexual health needs, assess safeguarding where relevant, and arrange appropriate follow-up and safety-netting.”

Especially for FGM

“I would provide non-judgemental, trauma-informed care, establish the patient's wishes, assess for physical and psychosexual complications, consider deinfibulation where indicated, and follow local safeguarding and legal requirements.”

Especially for adolescent stations

“I would maintain confidentiality within safeguarding limits, involve the young person in decisions according to their capacity, and avoid unnecessary intimate examination.”

Especially for PMB

“My priority is to exclude endometrial malignancy while also considering benign causes such as atrophy and polyps.”

Especially for HRT

“I would individualise the route, dose and regimen according to uterine status, menopausal stage, symptoms, VTE/cardiovascular and breast/endometrial risk, and review treatment regularly.”

The RCOG's current menopause material emphasises individualised HRT decisions, benefits/risks, and alternatives rather than an arbitrary universal treatment duration.
