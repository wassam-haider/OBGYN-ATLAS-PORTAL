SECTION 1

PART I — UTERINE CONDITIONS

1

Fibroids in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐

Problems

Pain/red degeneration

Rapid apparent growth

Pressure symptoms

Malpresentation

Obstructed labour

Placental abnormalities

Fetal growth restriction in selected cases

Preterm birth

Caesarean delivery

Postpartum haemorrhage

Retained placenta

Difficult caesarean if fibroid obstructs lower segment

Assessment

History: pain, bleeding, pressure symptoms

Abdominal examination

Ultrasound:

number

size

site

relation to placenta

relation to cervix/lower segment

fetal growth

MRI only when ultrasound is inadequate or anatomy needs clarification.

Management

Asymptomatic/small: expectant management.

Pain/red degeneration: rest, hydration and appropriate analgesia; exclude important differentials such as abruption, torsion and labour.

Large/multiple fibroids: consultant-led antenatal surveillance.

Consider serial fetal growth assessment where clinically indicated.

Document whether a lower-segment fibroid could obstruct labour.

Mode of birth is individualized.

Caesarean may be required if the fibroid obstructs the birth canal or creates another obstetric indication.

Myomectomy during pregnancy is generally avoided; surgery is reserved for exceptional situations such as an acute surgical abdomen or severe refractory complications.

Exam pearl:

Fibroid ≠ automatic Caesarean.

RCOG identifies fibroids as a major benign gynaecological condition and has recently highlighted the importance of improved pathways for fibroid care.

2

Adenomyosis in pregnancy

Potential issues

Pain

Miscarriage

Preterm birth

Placental complications

Abnormal placentation

Fetal growth problems in some patients

Increased obstetric intervention

Management

Usually expectant.

Specialist obstetric assessment if extensive adenomyosis.

Antenatal surveillance according to associated obstetric risk factors.

No routine surgical treatment during pregnancy.

3

Congenital uterine anomalies

Includes:

Septate uterus

Bicornuate uterus

Unicornuate uterus

Didelphys

Arcuate uterus

Cervical anomalies

Pregnancy risks

Miscarriage

Preterm birth

Malpresentation

Fetal growth restriction

Cervical insufficiency

Placental abnormalities

Caesarean delivery

Management

Confirm anatomy with ultrasound ± MRI if required.

Assess cervical length where clinically appropriate.

Monitor fetal growth.

Presentation determines delivery planning.

Previous reconstructive surgery must be considered.

Important
Do not confuse a uterine anomaly with fibroids or adenomyosis.
4

Previous myomectomy

This deserves its own category.

Assess:

Number of fibroids removed

Depth of uterine incision

Whether endometrial cavity was entered

Surgical approach

Operative report if available

Why it matters

Uterine rupture risk

Placenta praevia/accreta spectrum

Delivery planning

Management

Obtain operative notes where possible.

Individualized mode and timing of birth.

If significant full-thickness uterine surgery/cavity entry has occurred, planned Caesarean may be recommended rather than labour.

5

Endometrial polyps discovered during pregnancy

Usually incidental.

Management

Usually observation.

Avoid hysteroscopic polypectomy during pregnancy unless an exceptional indication exists.

Reassess postpartum if persistent/symptomatic.

6

Endometrial hyperplasia diagnosed before pregnancy

Pregnancy generally interrupts the usual hormonal environment.

Management

Review previous histology.

Establish whether atypia/EIN or malignancy was present.

Involve gynaecological oncology if there is atypia or suspected malignancy.

Definitive treatment is generally deferred until after pregnancy unless cancer is suspected.

SECTION 2

PART II — CERVICAL CONDITIONS

7

Cervical polyp in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐

Presentation:

Postcoital bleeding

Vaginal bleeding

Discharge

Incidental finding

Assessment

Speculum examination

Characterize lesion

Determine whether it appears benign

Consider cervical screening history.

Management
➔ Small, asymptomatic, apparently benign polyp → usually observe.
➔ Significant bleeding, large lesion, atypical appearance → senior gynaecological review.

Histology may be necessary if malignancy cannot be excluded.

Avoid unnecessary removal because cervical polypectomy can cause bleeding and may be associated with pregnancy complications.

8

Cervical ectropion

Common in pregnancy because of hormonal changes.

Presentation

Contact bleeding

Increased discharge

Management

Usually reassurance.

Exclude infection/cervical pathology when indicated.

Treatment is generally unnecessary during pregnancy.

9

Cervical insufficiency

This is a gynaecological/cervical structural problem with major obstetric consequences.

Think when

Previous painless second-trimester loss

Previous spontaneous very preterm birth

Short cervix

Cervical dilatation without labour

Management

Cervical-length surveillance where indicated.

Vaginal progesterone and/or cerclage according to clinical history and cervical findings.

Emergency/rescue cerclage in selected women with cervical dilatation, after excluding labour, infection and membrane rupture.

10

Cervical stenosis

Usually pre-existing.

Potential pregnancy relevance

Usually little effect on pregnancy.

May complicate cervical assessment or procedures.

Labour management depends on obstetric circumstances.

SECTION 3

PART III — OVARIAN CONDITIONS

11

Ovarian cyst in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐

This is one of the most important topics.

Types

Functional

Corpus luteum

Simple cyst

Benign neoplasms

Dermoid/teratoma

Cystadenoma

Endometrioma

Malignant

Epithelial ovarian cancer

Germ-cell tumour

Sex-cord stromal tumour

Metastatic tumour

RCOG's gynaecological curriculum specifically requires knowledge of functional cysts, dermoids, endometriotic cysts and ovarian cyst accidents.

Assessment

Ultrasound:

Size

Simple vs complex

Solid components

Papillary projections

Septations

Bilaterality

Doppler characteristics

Ascites

Other pelvic abnormalities

Management

Simple, benign-appearing, asymptomatic

➔ → expectant management.

Complex/suspicious

➔ → specialist assessment.

Acute abdomen

Think:

torsion

rupture

haemorrhage

infection

➔ → urgent gynaecology review.
12

Ovarian torsion in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐

Presentation

Sudden unilateral pelvic/abdominal pain

Nausea/vomiting

Adnexal tenderness

Diagnosis

Ultrasound ± Doppler.

Normal Doppler flow does not reliably exclude torsion.

Management

Surgical emergency.

Prompt laparoscopy/laparotomy depending on gestation, anatomy and clinical situation.

Aim for ovarian conservation where feasible.

13

Ruptured ovarian cyst

Presentation

Acute pelvic pain

Possible haemoperitoneum

Haemodynamic instability in severe cases

Management

ABC/resuscitation

FBC, group & save/crossmatch as indicated

Ultrasound

Exclude ectopic pregnancy in early pregnancy

Conservative if stable and bleeding is limited

Surgery if unstable or significant ongoing haemorrhage.

14

Endometrioma in pregnancy

Usually managed conservatively.

Problems

Pain

Torsion

Rupture

Rare malignant transformation

Management

Ultrasound surveillance if appropriate.

Surgery only when clinically indicated, especially acute complications or significant suspicion of malignancy.

RCOG describes ovarian endometriomas as part of endometriosis and notes that management depends partly on whether pregnancy is planned.

15

Mature cystic teratoma / dermoid

Usually benign.

Management

Observe if asymptomatic and benign-appearing.

Surgery if:

torsion

rupture

obstruction

significant growth

suspicious features.

16

Suspected ovarian malignancy in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Red flags:

Complex mass

Solid components

Papillary projections

Bilateral masses

Ascites

Rapid growth

Elevated tumour markers interpreted cautiously in pregnancy.

Management

Specialist gynaecological oncology referral.

Expert ultrasound.

MRI without gadolinium when clinically appropriate.

MDT discussion.

Decide:

surveillance

surgery

chemotherapy

delivery timing

Do not delay evaluation simply because the patient is pregnant.

RCOG's curriculum specifically includes ovarian, cervical, endometrial and vulval malignancy.

SECTION 4

PART IV — ENDOMETRIOSIS

17

Endometriosis in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐

Pregnancy does not cure endometriosis.

Possible complications:

Pain

Endometrioma complications

Torsion

Rupture

Rare bowel/bladder complications

Rare spontaneous haemoperitoneum

Management

Usually conservative.

Analgesia appropriate for pregnancy.

Avoid hormonal suppression because pregnancy itself is established.

Surgery only for significant complications or malignancy concern.

RCOG notes that endometriosis can involve the ovaries, bowel, bladder and other pelvic structures and that extensive disease may require multidisciplinary specialist care.

SECTION 5

PART V — PELVIC ORGAN PROLAPSE

18

Uterovaginal prolapse in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐

Rare but important.

Types:

Uterine prolapse

Cervical prolapse

Vaginal wall prolapse

Cystocele

Rectocele

Enterocele

Problems

Vaginal ulceration

Bleeding

Infection

Urinary retention

Constipation

Cervical oedema

Preterm labour

Obstructed labour

Difficulty reducing prolapse

Management

Mild/asymptomatic

➔ → observation.

Symptomatic

Pelvic examination

Treat ulceration/infection if present

Vaginal pessary may be used in selected patients.

Monitor cervical changes and urinary symptoms.

Assess fetal growth and preterm-birth risk according to clinical situation.

Severe prolapse

➔ → consultant-led management.

Delivery

Vaginal birth may be possible.

Caesarean is not automatically required.

Caesarean may be necessary if prolapse is irreducible and obstructs labour or another obstetric indication exists.

RCOG's core curriculum explicitly includes uterovaginal prolapse and urogynaecological problems.

19

Cystocele in pregnancy

Management

Conservative.

Treat urinary retention/UTI.

Pessary in selected symptomatic patients.

Delivery usually based on obstetric indications.

20

Rectocele/enterocele in pregnancy

Usually conservative.

Treat:

Constipation

Pelvic pressure

Defaecatory dysfunction

Surgery is generally postponed until postpartum.

SECTION 6

PART VI — VULVAL & VAGINAL CONDITIONS

21

Bartholin cyst/abscess

Pregnancy-specific approach

Cyst:

➔ → conservative if asymptomatic.

Abscess:

Antibiotics when indicated.

Drainage if clinically necessary.

Word catheter is commonly used.

Avoid unnecessary extensive surgery during pregnancy.

Important differential

Vulval malignancy, particularly in an atypical lesion.

22

Vulval varicosities

May occur due to pelvic venous congestion.

Management

Reassurance

Avoid prolonged standing

Support garments

Analgesia if necessary

Usually regress postpartum.

Important
thrombosis can occur and should be assessed if painful, hard or asymmetric.
23

Vulval/vaginal haematoma

Consider:

Trauma

Delivery-related injury

Spontaneous haematoma

Management

Assess haemodynamic status.

Examine extent.

Analgesia.

Drainage/surgery if expanding, infected or causing significant symptoms/haemodynamic compromise.

24

Lichen sclerosus in pregnancy

Usually does not require major alteration of pregnancy care.

Management

Continue appropriate topical corticosteroid therapy.

Monitor for fissuring/trauma.

Ensure malignancy surveillance.

Vaginal delivery is usually possible.

25

Vulval intraepithelial neoplasia

Management

Specialist vulval disease assessment.

Biopsy suspicious lesions.

Conservative surveillance can be appropriate.

Definitive excision is generally individualized and often deferred when safe.
SECTION 7

PART VII — GYNAECOLOGICAL CANCER IN PREGNANCY

This is a major MRCOG topic.

26

Cervical cancer in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Possible presentation:

Postcoital bleeding

Abnormal bleeding

Abnormal cervical appearance

Abnormal screening result

Incidental diagnosis

Initial assessment

Speculum examination

Cervical biopsy when indicated

Histopathology

MRI without gadolinium when staging requires it

MDT referral

Management depends on

Gestational age

Histological type

Stage

Tumour size

Lymph-node status

Patient's wishes

Desire for continuation of pregnancy

Principle:

Do not automatically terminate pregnancy.

Early-stage disease may sometimes be managed while continuing pregnancy, whereas advanced disease may require treatment that cannot safely be delayed.

MDT

Obstetrician

Gynaecological oncologist

Medical oncologist

Radiation oncologist

Neonatologist

Anaesthetist

Specialist midwife

Pathologist/radiologist

27

Endometrial cancer in pregnancy

Extremely uncommon because pregnancy itself is unusual in women with established endometrial carcinoma.

Management

Gynaecological oncology MDT.

Confirm histology.

Determine stage.

Individualize pregnancy continuation versus definitive cancer treatment.

Definitive surgery is generally postpartum if pregnancy can safely continue.
28. Ovarian cancer in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Approach

➔ Suspicious adnexal mass → specialist ultrasound → MDT.

Possible options:

Conservative surveillance

For clearly benign lesions.

Surgery

For:

Suspicious malignancy

Acute complications

Significant growth

Torsion/rupture

Timing is individualized, often aiming for the second trimester when surgery is necessary and pregnancy can safely continue.

Chemotherapy

Some regimens can be used during pregnancy when necessary, generally after the first trimester.

Avoid chemotherapy in the first trimester because of major fetal teratogenic risk.

29

Vulval cancer in pregnancy

Rare.

Management

Biopsy suspicious lesion.

Gynaecological oncology MDT.

Surgical treatment may be necessary during pregnancy depending on stage.

Groin-node assessment and treatment depend on tumour characteristics.

30

Vaginal cancer in pregnancy

Rare.

Management requires:

Biopsy

MRI/staging

MDT

Individualized timing of treatment and delivery.

Radiotherapy is generally incompatible with continuing pregnancy.

SECTION 8

PART VIII — INFECTIONS THAT ARE PRIMARILY GYNAECOLOGICAL

31

Bacterial vaginosis

Pregnancy relevance:

Symptomatic infection

Association with preterm birth

Management

Treat symptomatic women according to pregnancy-safe antibiotic guidance.

Routine treatment of asymptomatic BV solely to prevent preterm birth is not generally recommended.

32

Vulvovaginal candidiasis

Very common in pregnancy.

Management

Prefer topical azole therapy.

Avoid oral fluconazole where possible in pregnancy.

33

Trichomoniasis

Management

Test and treat appropriately.

Treat sexual partner(s).

Screen for other STIs.

Use pregnancy-compatible treatment.

34

Chlamydia

Important because of

Maternal infection

Neonatal conjunctivitis

Neonatal pneumonia

Management

Pregnancy-safe antibiotic treatment.

Partner management.

Test of cure where recommended.

STI screening.

35

Gonorrhoea

Risk of:

Maternal pelvic infection

Neonatal ophthalmia

Disseminated infection

Requires appropriate pregnancy-safe antibiotic treatment and partner notification.

36

Syphilis in pregnancy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

This becomes an obstetric + infectious disease problem, but is essential in a gynaecological pregnancy atlas.

Management

Immediate appropriate penicillin therapy.

Stage disease.

Treat partner(s).

Maternal serological follow-up.

Fetal assessment.

37

Genital herpes

Primary infection late in pregnancy is particularly important.

Management depends on:

Primary vs recurrent infection

Gestational age

Lesions/prodrome at labour

Antiviral treatment may be indicated.

Delivery planning depends on active lesions/prodrome and clinical circumstances.

PART IX — URINARY/GYNAECOLOGICAL PROBLEMS

38

Stress urinary incontinence in pregnancy

Usually conservative:

Pelvic-floor muscle training

Lifestyle measures

Treat constipation

Avoid unnecessary surgery

39

Overactive bladder

Behavioural modification

Bladder training

Fluid/caffeine advice

Pelvic-floor therapy

Drug treatment is generally avoided unless specialist assessment indicates benefit.

40

Urinary retention due to prolapse

This is an important emergency complication.

Management

Bladder scan

Catheterization if necessary

Reduce prolapse

Treat precipitating factors

Pessary if appropriate

Monitor renal function if prolonged obstruction.

PART X — GYNAECOLOGICAL CONDITIONS RELATED TO PREVIOUS SURGERY

41

Previous cervical surgery

Examples:

LLETZ/LEEP

Cone biopsy

Multiple cervical procedures

Pregnancy implications:

Cervical shortening

Preterm birth

Cervical stenosis

Cervical incompetence in selected patients

Management involves appropriate cervical assessment and preterm-birth prevention strategy based on individual history.

42

Previous endometriosis surgery

Consider:

Adhesions

Distorted pelvic anatomy

Previous bowel/bladder surgery

Caesarean surgical complexity

Important during abdominal surgery.
43

Previous ovarian surgery

Possible:

Reduced ovarian reserve

Adhesions

Altered anatomy

Increased risk of surgical injury

Important when planning Caesarean or other pelvic surgery.
44

Previous extensive pelvic surgery

May result in:

Adhesions

Bowel injury

Bladder injury

Ureteric injury

Difficult Caesarean

Management

Review operative history.

Consider senior surgeon involvement.

Urology/general surgery backup if major adhesions or organ involvement is anticipated.

PART XI — OTHER IMPORTANT GYNAECOLOGICAL CONDITIONS

45

PCOS in pregnancy

Risks include:

GDM

hypertensive disorders

miscarriage

preterm birth

metabolic complications

Management becomes primarily obstetric/metabolic.

46

Endometriosis-associated pregnancy

Consider:

preterm birth

placenta praevia

hypertensive disorders

rare spontaneous haemoperitoneum.

47

Severe pelvic adhesions

Important particularly with

previous Caesarean

endometriosis

previous PID

previous laparotomy.

Main concern = operative difficulty and visceral injury.

48

Pelvic inflammatory disease discovered in pregnancy

True PID is uncommon once pregnancy is established.

If pelvic pain/fever occurs, consider:

appendicitis

UTI/pyelonephritis

chorioamnionitis

ovarian torsion

ectopic/heterotopic pregnancy in early pregnancy

tubo-ovarian abscess.

49

Tubo-ovarian abscess

Rare but potentially serious.

Management

Antibiotics appropriate for pregnancy.

MDT involvement.

Drainage/surgery if abscess is large, ruptured, refractory or causing sepsis.

50

Genital tract congenital anomalies

Examples:

Vaginal septum

Transverse vaginal septum

Cervical agenesis/abnormality

Uterine anomalies

Mullerian anomalies

Management is individualized according to anatomy and obstruction.

THE HIGH-YIELD MRCOG MASTER CLASSIFICATION

For examination purposes, remember pregnancy-related gynaecological disease under 10 headings:

Category Conditions
1. Uterus Fibroid, adenomyosis, uterine anomaly, previous myomectomy, endometrial polyp/hyperplasia
2. Cervix Cervical polyp, ectropion, cervical insufficiency, previous LLETZ/cone
3. Ovary Simple cyst, dermoid, endometrioma, cystadenoma, torsion, rupture, ovarian cancer
4. Endometriosis Pelvic, ovarian, bowel, bladder disease
5. Prolapse Uterine prolapse, cystocele, rectocele, enterocele
6. Vulva/vagina Bartholin disease, vulval varicosities, lichen sclerosus, VIN, vaginal lesions
7. Cancer Cervical, ovarian, endometrial, vulval, vaginal
8. Infection BV, candidiasis, trichomoniasis, chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, herpes
9. Urogynaecology SUI, OAB, urinary retention, prolapse-related obstruction
10. Previous surgery/adhesions Myomectomy, LLETZ, cone, ovarian surgery, endometriosis surgery, laparotomy

UNIVERSAL RCOG APPROACH TO ANY GYNAECOLOGICAL CONDITION IN PREGNANCY

For every case, use this sequence:

STEP 1 — Is mother stable?

ABC

BP

pulse

RR

temperature

oxygen saturation

pain

bleeding

sepsis

STEP 2 — Is fetus well?

Depending on gestation:

fetal heart

CTG

ultrasound

growth

liquor

placenta

Dopplers where indicated.

STEP 3 — Identify the gynaecological pathology

➔ History → examination → ultrasound → appropriate MRI/biopsy/laboratory testing.

STEP 4 — Decide whether it is:

A

Incidental and benign

➔ → observe.
B

Symptomatic but stable

➔ → conservative/specialist management.
C

Acute surgical condition

➔ → urgent surgery.
D

Suspicious for malignancy

➔ → gynaecological oncology MDT.
E

Infection/sepsis

➔ → cultures + pregnancy-safe antimicrobial treatment + source control.

STEP 5 — Ask the five pregnancy questions

Does it threaten the mother?

Does it threaten the fetus?

Does it obstruct labour?

Does it require surgery now or can surgery wait?

Does it change timing/mode of delivery?

STEP 6 — MDT when appropriate

Particularly:

Gynaecologist + obstetrician + maternal-fetal medicine + gynaecological oncologist + radiologist + pathologist + anaesthetist + neonatologist, with other specialties according to the organ involved.
