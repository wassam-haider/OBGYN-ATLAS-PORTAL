SECTION 1

Overview & Core Guidance

You are not trying to prove that you know everything. You are demonstrating that you are a safe, competent, compassionate ST5-level obstetrician & gynaecologist who can apply knowledge, communicate clearly and make appropriate decisions under pressure.

The current RCOG format is 14 tasks, each 12 minutes, including 2 minutes reading/preparation and 10 minutes of active assessment. The two task types are simulated patient/colleague and structured discussion. The five domains are patient safety, communication with patients/relatives, communication with colleagues, information gathering and applied clinical knowledge.

SECTION 2

1. THE FIVE DOMAINS — YOUR MASTER CHECKLIST

Every answer should subconsciously cover these five areas:

① Patient safety

Ask yourself:

Is this patient stable?

Is there an immediate life-threatening problem?

What must happen now?

Who needs to know?

What could go wrong?

What monitoring is required?

What is my escalation plan?

What is my safety-net?

② Communication with patient/family

Demonstrate:

Introduction

Rapport

Open questions

Active listening

Empathy

Responding to cues

Plain English

Checking understanding

Shared decision-making

Consent

Autonomy

Safety-netting

③ Communication with colleagues

Demonstrate:

Respect

Clear delegation

Appropriate escalation

Closed-loop communication

Structured handover

SBAR

Appropriate consultant involvement

④ Information gathering

Don't take an exhaustive history.

Take a focused history relevant to the task.

Think:

What information would change my management?

⑤ Applied clinical knowledge

Don't merely list facts.

Explain:

➔ Finding → interpretation → significance → action.

This distinction is extremely important.

The RCOG specifically describes the assessment as testing application of knowledge rather than isolated knowledge recall.

SECTION 3

2. THE 12-MINUTE STATION STRATEGY

Current timing

Time What you do
0–2 min Read/preparation
2–3 min Introduction + agenda
3–6 min Information gathering / patient's concerns
6–9 min Explain/assess/options
9–11 min Shared decision / management / safety
11–12 min Summary + safety-net + final question

The exact division will vary depending on the station.

Do not rigidly force every station into these timings.

SECTION 5

4. YOUR FIRST 30 SECONDS

A strong opening:

"Hello, I'm Dr ___, one of the doctors looking after you. I'd like to understand what has been happening, discuss the options with you and make sure we agree on a safe plan. Is that okay?"

Then:

"Before we start, is there anything in particular you're most worried about?"

This immediately demonstrates:

introduction

role

purpose

patient-centred approach

agenda

concern identification

The RCOG's communication framework specifically emphasises introduction, rapport, focused questioning, empathy, active listening, manageable information, shared decision-making, consent and dignity.

SECTION 6

5. SIMULATED PATIENT — THE GOLDEN STRUCTURE

Use:

➔ ICE → INFO → OPTIONS → DECISION → SAFETY

ICE

I — Ideas

"What is your understanding of what is happening?"

C — Concerns

"What is worrying you most?"

E — Expectations

"What were you hoping we could achieve today?"

6

SIMULATED PATIENT: OPEN → FOCUSED → CLOSED

Start:

"Can you tell me what has been happening?"

Then focus:

"When did the pain start?"

Then close:

"Is the pain constant?"

Avoid interrogating the patient with:

"Pain? Fever? Bleeding? Vomiting? Urine?"

That sounds robotic.

SECTION 7

7. ACTIVE LISTENING

Use:

Verbal

"I see."

"Tell me more about that."

"That sounds difficult."

"What happened next?"

Non-verbal

Appropriate eye contact

Don't interrupt

Open posture

Nod appropriately

Calm tone

Reflecting

Patient:

"I'm terrified this means I'll never have another baby."

You:

"It sounds as though your biggest concern is what this might mean for your future fertility."

Excellent communication.

SECTION 8

8. EMPATHY — DON'T JUST SAY "I'M SORRY"

Weak:

"I'm sorry."

Better:

"I can see that this has been very frightening for you."

Then pause.

Then:

"Would you like to tell me a little more about what worries you?"

Empathy formula

➔ Acknowledge → validate → support

"I can see this is very upsetting. Anyone in this situation could feel overwhelmed. We'll go through this together and I'll explain what we know and what we still need to find out."

SECTION 9

9. RESPONDING TO EMOTIONAL CUES

Patient:

"Is this because I did something wrong?"

Don't immediately explain pathology.

Say:

"It sounds like you're blaming yourself."

Then:

"There is nothing you've told me that suggests you caused this."

Then explain.

SECTION 11

11. CLOSED-LOOP COMMUNICATION

Extremely important in emergencies.

Instead of:

"Someone get blood."

Say:

"Sarah, please activate the major haemorrhage protocol and tell me when blood products are available."

Then:

"Can you confirm that has been activated?"

This demonstrates safe teamworking.

SECTION 12

12. STRUCTURED DISCUSSION

This is essentially a consultant-style discussion.

The examiner may ask:

"What would you do?"

Don't give a random list.

Use:

➔ ASSESS → DIAGNOSE → STABILISE → INVESTIGATE → MANAGE → ESCALATE → REVIEW

Example:

"First I would assess ABCDE and determine whether she is clinically stable. I would then establish the likely diagnosis and differential diagnoses. I would initiate appropriate investigations while treating immediately reversible problems. I would involve the senior team early if there are concerns about deterioration and reassess her response to treatment."

Then give specifics.

SECTION 14

15. NUMERICAL VALUES — HOW TO USE THEM

MRCOG Part 3 is not a "numbers dumping" exam.

Use numbers when they influence a decision.

For example:

"Her blood pressure is 170/110 mmHg, which is severe hypertension and requires urgent assessment and treatment."

Better than:

"Normal BP is 120/80."

Useful emergency thresholds to know

Parameter Important value
Severe hypertension ≥160 systolic or ≥110 diastolic mmHg
Maternal tachycardia Usually >100–120/min, interpret clinically
Maternal hypoxia SpO₂ <94% generally concerning
Fever ≥38°C
Oliguria <0.5 mL/kg/hour
Significant PPH ≥1000 mL with ongoing bleeding
Major PPH ≥2500 mL
Hypoglycaemia <4.0 mmol/L often triggers treatment in pregnancy
Severe thrombocytopenia <50 ×10⁹/L particularly important around procedures/bleeding
Platelets in pre-eclampsia <100 ×10⁹/L is a severe-feature laboratory abnormality
Proteinuria PCR ≥30 mg/mmol or ACR ≥8 mg/mmol
Normal adult HR approximately 60–100/min
Normal adult respiratory rate approximately 12–20/min
Important
Always use the clinical context and local guideline/laboratory reference ranges rather than treating a number in isolation.

SECTION 16

17. BREAKING BAD NEWS — NEVER DO THESE

❌ "I have some bad news."

Then immediately unload 10 facts.

❌ "Unfortunately your baby is dead."

Better:

"I'm afraid the scan has confirmed that your baby has died."

Then STOP.

Allow silence.

Don't immediately fill the silence.

SECTION 20

21. MATERIAL RISKS

Don't just recite generic risks.

Ask:

"Are there any particular concerns you have about the procedure?"

And tailor risks.

For example, before hysterectomy:

bleeding

transfusion

infection

injury to bladder

ureteric injury

bowel injury

thromboembolism

anaesthetic complications

conversion of surgical approach

ovarian consequences depending on procedure

impact on fertility

SECTION 21

22. NUMERICAL RISK COMMUNICATION

When a specific numerical risk is known, communicate it naturally.

Example:

"The risk of ureteric injury is approximately X%, although your individual risk may be higher because of..."

Then:

"Would you like me to explain what that means in practical terms?"

Avoid:

"There is a 0.2% chance."

Then moving on.

The patient may not understand what that means.

Instead:

"That is approximately 2 people in 1000."

Golden rule

Absolute risk > relative risk

Instead of:

"This doubles the risk."

Say:

"The risk increases from approximately 1 in 1000 to 2 in 1000."

24. DON'T PUSH YOUR PREFERRED OPTION

Weak:

"I think you should have surgery."

SECTION 25

26. RISK COMMUNICATION — THE 7-STEP MODEL

1

Establish baseline

"The overall risk is low."

2

Give absolute risk

"Approximately 1 in 1000."

3

Explain meaning

"So approximately one person in every thousand undergoing this procedure."

4

Individualise

"Your risk may be different because..."

5

Balance

"The procedure also has important benefits..."

6

Compare alternatives

7

Check understanding

"Does that make sense?"

27

WHEN THE PATIENT ASKS "WHAT WOULD YOU DO?"

Don't say:

"I'd have surgery."

Instead:

"If I were in your position, I would want to understand the benefits, risks and alternatives. Based on the information you've given me, medically I would lean towards X because..."

This preserves professional boundaries and autonomy.

SECTION 27

29. MDT COMMUNICATION

Use:

➔ Problem → Evidence → Question → Recommendation

Example:

"This is a 42-year-old with an adnexal mass and concerning imaging features. The CA-125 is elevated and imaging suggests possible malignancy. I would like the MDT to review the imaging and agree whether she requires primary surgery or further diagnostic assessment."

SECTION 28

30. SBAR — MUST KNOW

S — Situation

"I'm Dr X, the O&G registrar. I'm calling about a 34-week pregnant woman with severe hypertension and neurological symptoms."

B — Background

"She has chronic hypertension and is currently admitted with suspected pre-eclampsia."

A — Assessment

"Her BP is 170/112, she has severe headache and brisk reflexes. CTG is currently reassuring."

R — Recommendation

"I would like you to review her urgently. I am starting immediate management for severe hypertension and magnesium sulfate is being considered according to the clinical picture."

SECTION 29

31. SBAR — NEVER FORGET THE "R"

Many candidates give:

Situation

Background

Assessment

Then stop.

The consultant needs:

"What do you want me to do?"

Always end with a clear recommendation.

SECTION 34

36. WHEN TO CALL THE CONSULTANT

Don't wait until you've completed your entire differential.

Escalate early for:

maternal instability

severe haemorrhage

eclampsia

suspected uterine rupture

major sepsis

difficult operative decision

major operative complication

unexpected deterioration

uncertainty involving patient safety

conflict with patient/relative

safeguarding concern

significant ethical/legal issue

Excellent phrase:

"I would rather escalate early than delay senior input when there is a potential threat to maternal or fetal safety."

SECTION 35

37. "I DON'T KNOW" — HOW TO SURVIVE A VIVA

Never invent.

Say:

"I don't recall the exact figure, but the clinically important threshold is..."

Or:

"I'm not certain of the exact numerical value, so I would check the current local guideline. My immediate management would be..."

This is much safer than giving an incorrect number confidently.

SECTION 36

38. EXAMINER VIVA — WHEN YOU GET STUCK

Use:

"Let me structure my answer."

Then:

"I would consider this under immediate management, investigation, definitive treatment and follow-up."

This buys you thinking time without appearing lost.

SECTION 37

39. IF THE EXAMINER INTERRUPTS

STOP.

Listen.

Answer exactly what was asked.

Don't continue your rehearsed speech.

The examiner may be deliberately moving you to another domain.

SECTION 38

"That changes my immediate priority. This is severe hypertension and requires urgent treatment and senior review."

SECTION 44

47. SHARED DECISION-MAKING — EXAMINER PHRASES

Useful phrases:

"There isn't necessarily one correct option."

"There are benefits and disadvantages to each approach."

"I'd like to understand what matters most to you."

"Would you like some time to consider this?"

"Would you like your partner/family member involved?"

"What questions do you have?"

SECTION 45

48. THE "PATIENT-FRIENDLY LANGUAGE" RULE

Avoid:

❌ "PPROM"

Say:

"Your waters have broken earlier than expected."

Avoid:

❌ "IUFD"

Say:

"Sadly, the baby has died."

Avoid:

❌ "Malignant"

Say:

"Cancerous."

Avoid:

❌ "EUA"

Say:

"An examination under anaesthetic."

Avoid:

❌ "MUA"

Say:

"A procedure to remove remaining pregnancy tissue from the womb."

The RCOG specifically expects information to be delivered in manageable amounts using patient-friendly language and avoiding unexplained jargon.

SECTION 46

49. THE "CHUNK AND CHECK" TECHNIQUE

Don't give a 3-minute monologue.

Chunk 1

"The scan has shown a pregnancy outside the womb."

Pause.

Check

"Is that clear so far?"

Chunk 2

"This is called an ectopic pregnancy."

Pause.

Check

"Would you like me to explain how we normally treat this?"

This demonstrates communication rather than information dumping.

SECTION 47

50. HOW TO EXPLAIN A COMPLEX MEDICAL ISSUE

Use:

➔ WHAT → WHY → OPTIONS → RISKS → PLAN

Example:

"The problem is X."

"This is important because Y."

"We have three possible approaches."

"Each has benefits and risks."

"Considering your circumstances, I would recommend X."

"However, the final decision is yours."

51

"WHY?" QUESTIONS FROM EXAMINER

Examiner:

"Why would you do that?"

Don't repeat the management.

Explain the reasoning.

"Because the immediate priority is to prevent maternal deterioration."

"Because this result changes the risk-benefit balance."

"Because delaying treatment could result in significant morbidity."

52

"WHAT ARE THE RISKS?"

Use:

Common

Serious

Procedure-specific

Patient-specific

Example:

"Common risks include pain and infection. Serious risks include haemorrhage and injury to surrounding organs. In this patient, the risk may be increased because of previous surgery."

53

"WHAT ARE THE BENEFITS?"

Don't say:

"It will cure her."

Instead:

symptom improvement

disease control

prevention of complications

diagnostic information

improved quality of life

survival benefit where relevant

54

"WHAT ARE THE ALTERNATIVES?"

Always consider:

A — Active treatment

B — Alternative treatment

C — Conservative/expectant management

D — No treatment

Then explain consequences.

SECTION 48

55. CLINICAL REASONING — HIGH-SCORING FORMAT

When presenting a diagnosis:

"My leading diagnosis is X because of A, B and C."

"The important differential is Y because of D."

"The diagnosis I particularly need to exclude is Z because it is potentially life-threatening."

This is much stronger than:

"Differentials are A, B, C, D, E, F..."

SECTION 49

56. WHEN YOU HAVE LIMITED INFORMATION

Say:

"I would first establish whether she is clinically stable."

Then:

"My immediate management would not wait for all investigations if she is unstable."

Excellent patient-safety thinking.

SECTION 50

57. WHEN THERE IS A CONFLICT BETWEEN PATIENT AND DOCTOR

Use:

➔ Explore → Understand → Explain → Negotiate → Respect → Safety-net

Never become authoritarian.

SECTION 51

58. WHEN PATIENT WANTS AN UNNECESSARY TEST

Don't say:

"No, you don't need it."

Say:

"I understand why you'd like the test. Let me explain what information it would and wouldn't give us, and whether it would change what we do."

Then discuss:

benefits

harms

false positives

false reassurance

alternatives

cost/resources where relevant

SECTION 52

59. WHEN PATIENT WANTS AN UNAVAILABLE TREATMENT

Acknowledge:

"I understand why that option is attractive."

Then:

"Unfortunately, I don't think it would be safe/appropriate in your circumstances because..."

Offer alternative.

SECTION 53

60. DEALING WITH COMPLAINTS

Use:

Listen

Don't interrupt.

Acknowledge

"I can see why you're unhappy."

Apologise when appropriate

"I'm sorry that you experienced this."

An apology does not necessarily mean accepting blame.

Explain

Only after listening.

Action

"I'll make sure this is discussed with the senior team."

Follow-up

Provide appropriate complaint pathway.

SECTION 54

61. TEAM CONFLICT

If a colleague disagrees:

"I appreciate your concern."

"Can you explain your reasoning?"

Then:

"My concern is patient safety because..."

If unresolved:

"I think we should involve the senior clinician."

Never humiliate a colleague.

SECTION 55

62. JUNIOR DOCTOR MAKES A MISTAKE

Priorities:

Patient safety

Stabilise patient

Escalate

Honest communication

Documentation

Learning/support

Don't start by blaming the junior.

SECTION 56

63. DISCLOSING AN ERROR

Say:

"I would be open and honest with the patient about what happened."

Explain:

what happened

current consequences

what has been done

what will happen next

appropriate apology

support

investigation/governance

Avoid:

❌ blaming colleagues

❌ speculation

❌ hiding information

SECTION 57

64. ETHICAL SCENARIOS — FOUR PRINCIPLES

Remember

Autonomy

Patient's right to choose.

Beneficence

Act in patient's best interests.

Non-maleficence

Avoid harm.

Justice

Fair allocation and treatment.

But don't just name them.

Apply them:

"Although I believe treatment would be beneficial, she has capacity and is entitled to refuse it."

67. EMERGENCY NUMERICAL VALUES WORTH KNOWING

Shock

Don't wait for hypotension.

Important signs

tachycardia

altered mental state

prolonged capillary refill

oliguria

cool peripheries

rising lactate

Urine

Target commonly:

≥0.5 mL/kg/hour

≤2 seconds

94–98%

SECTION 64

72. COMMON MRCOG PART 3 FAILING BEHAVIOURS

❌ Knowledge dumping

You know the answer but don't answer the question.

❌ No empathy

Technically perfect but patient feels ignored.

❌ No patient agenda

You immediately start lecturing.

❌ Excessive jargon

Patient doesn't understand.

❌ No safety-net

You finish after giving the diagnosis.

❌ No escalation

You attempt to manage everything yourself.

❌ Delayed consultant involvement

Dangerous.

❌ Poor prioritisation

Treating routine issues before life-threatening ones.

❌ Giving false certainty

"Definitely."

Instead:

"The most likely diagnosis is..."

❌ Ignoring patient autonomy

"You need to have this."

❌ Inventing numerical values

If unsure, don't guess.

SECTION 66

74. THE MRCOG "EXAMINER WANTS TO WORK WITH YOU" TEST

Imagine the examiner is your consultant and asks:

"Would I trust this registrar with my labour ward?"

You should appear:

Calm + safe + organised + kind + decisive + appropriately humble.

Not:

Panicked + authoritarian + encyclopaedic + indecisive.

The RCOG's own description makes clear that the assessment integrates clinical knowledge with communication and patient safety rather than testing knowledge in isolation.
