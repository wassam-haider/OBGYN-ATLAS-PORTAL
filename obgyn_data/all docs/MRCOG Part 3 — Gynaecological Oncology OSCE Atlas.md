MRCOG Part 3 — Gynaecological Oncology OSCE Atlas

This is an OSCE-focused oncology guide, designed around what you should say, examine, measure, interpret, and propose at an MRCOG Part 3 station.

For MRCOG Part 3, do not merely recite staging. The examiner is usually assessing whether you can:

recognise a possible malignancy;
perform a focused but complete examination;
identify red flags;
interpret investigations;
establish histological diagnosis;
stage appropriately;
explain the diagnosis sensitively;
formulate an MDT plan;
discuss fertility, menopause, sexual function and psychosocial issues;
obtain informed consent;
recognise emergencies;
communicate uncertainty safely.

Current FIGO staging is particularly important: endometrial staging was substantially revised in 2023, including molecular classification.

A commonly used threshold in PMB pathways is:

≤4 mm

31. MOLECULAR CLASSIFICATION — VERY IMPORTANT

Four major molecular categories:

1. POLE-mutated

Generally excellent prognosis.

2. MMR-deficient

Associated with:

Lynch syndrome in some patients
immunotherapy relevance.
3. NSMP

No specific molecular profile.

4. p53-abnormal

Generally aggressive biology.

Molecular classification is encouraged in endometrial carcinoma because it can affect prognostic risk stratification and treatment decisions.

38. RMI — RISK OF MALIGNANCY INDEX

High-yield numerical OSCE formula:

RMI = U × M × CA-125

Where:

U = ultrasound score

Common scoring:

0 = no abnormal ultrasound features
1 = one abnormal feature
3 = ≥2 abnormal features.

Features include:

multilocular cyst
solid areas
metastases
ascites
bilateral lesions.
M = menopausal score
premenopausal = 1
postmenopausal = 3.
CA-125

Measured in U/mL.

RMI ≥200

Traditionally considered a high-risk threshold in many UK pathways and warrants specialist gynaecological oncology referral.

OSCE caution: local guidelines may use updated referral algorithms, so state that you would follow the current local pathway.

Say:

83. “ONCOLOGY EXAMINATION” — 2-MINUTE MRCOG SCRIPT

If the examiner says:

“Perform an examination of this patient with suspected gynaecological malignancy.”

Say:

“I would first assess the patient's general condition, vital signs, BMI and performance status. I would look for pallor, cachexia, jaundice, oedema and peripheral lymphadenopathy, particularly cervical, supraclavicular and inguinal nodes.”

Then:

“I would perform a full abdominal examination looking for distension, ascites, masses, hepatosplenomegaly and signs of peritoneal disease.”

Then:

“With consent and a chaperone, I would perform an intimate examination, initially inspecting the vulva and perineum for lesions, ulceration, pigmentation, induration or architectural distortion.”

Then:

“I would perform speculum examination to assess the vagina and cervix, looking for a tumour, ulceration, friability, bleeding or abnormal discharge.”

Then:

“I would perform bimanual examination assessing the size and mobility of the uterus, adnexal masses and pelvic fixation.”

Then:

“Where cervical, vaginal or advanced pelvic malignancy is suspected, I would perform a rectovaginal examination to assess the parametria, uterosacral ligaments, rectovaginal septum and pelvic sidewalls.”

Finally:

“I would document all findings precisely, including lesion size in centimetres, location, mobility, fixation and nodal findings, and correlate these with histology and appropriate imaging for staging.”

84. THE “CANCER STATION” MASTER FRAMEWORK

When given any gynaecological oncology case, think:

1. SUSPECT

What symptoms/risk factors suggest cancer?

↓

2. EXAMINE

General → nodes → abdomen → vulva → vagina → cervix → bimanual → rectovaginal.

↓

3. CONFIRM

Biopsy/histology

↓

4. STAGE

Clinical examination + imaging + pathology ± surgery.

↓

5. CLASSIFY

Histology + grade + molecular markers where relevant.

↓

6. MDT

Discuss specialist treatment.

↓

7. TREAT

Surgery / chemotherapy / radiotherapy / systemic therapy.

↓

8. PRESERVE

Fertility + ovarian function + sexual function.

↓

9. SUPPORT

Psychological + social + symptom control + CNS.

↓

10. FOLLOW-UP

Recurrence surveillance + late effects + rehabilitation.

🔴 MRCOG EXAMINER “GOLDEN POINTS”

If you remember only a few things, remember these:

Postmenopausal bleeding → exclude endometrial cancer.
Postcoital bleeding → consider cervical cancer.
Persistent bloating/early satiety → consider ovarian cancer.
Persistent vulval itch/ulcer → biopsy suspicious lesion.
A suspicious cervical lesion requires tissue diagnosis.
CA-125 is not a stand-alone diagnostic test.
RMI = U × M × CA-125.
RMI ≥200 is a traditional UK high-risk threshold.
Cervical cancer staging is heavily dependent on local extension and nodes.
Endometrial cancer now uses the 2023 FIGO system.
Endometrial molecular classification matters: POLE, MMRd, NSMP, p53abn.
GTD → follow β-hCG trends, not just a single value.
GTN FIGO score 0–6 = low risk; ≥7 = high risk.
Always ask about fertility before treatment in reproductive-age patients.
Always ask about sexual function and menopause after gynaecological cancer treatment.
Always mention MDT.
Do not give prognosis before stage/histology are known.
Cancer examination must be systematic and documented in centimetres.
Think about lymph nodes and distant metastases, not just the primary tumour.
In MRCOG Part 3, communication and patient-centred care are examined just as much as factual oncology knowledge.
