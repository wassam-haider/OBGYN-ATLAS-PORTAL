Overview & Core Guidance

➔ Maternal prognosis → fetal/neonatal prognosis → fertility prognosis → recurrence risk → long-term prognosis → mortality/morbidity.
SECTION 2

PART I — OBSTETRIC CONDITIONS

1

Hypertension in pregnancy

Condition Maternal prognosis Fetal prognosis Recurrence / long-term
Chronic hypertension Usually good if controlled; increased risk of superimposed PE, abruption, stroke ↑ FGR, preterm birth, stillbirth Recurs as chronic disease; ↑ later CV/renal risk
Gestational hypertension Usually favourable, but may progress to PE Usually good if term and uncomplicated; ↑ FGR/preterm risk ↑ recurrence of hypertensive disease
Mild pre-eclampsia Generally favourable with surveillance Usually good if late-onset Increased recurrence and future CV disease
Early-onset PE Higher maternal morbidity ↑ FGR, prematurity, stillbirth Recurrence substantially increased
Severe PE Potentially life-threatening: stroke, HELLP, renal/liver complications High prematurity/FGR risk Increased future hypertension/CVD
Eclampsia Usually recover with treatment, but risk of stroke, aspiration, organ failure and death Increased fetal hypoxia, prematurity and death Increased recurrence of PE/eclampsia
RCOG notes that prognosis is particularly dependent on gestational age
serious maternal/perinatal morbidity and mortality increase substantially when PE occurs before 33 weeks.
Important RCOG recurrence figures for PE
Previous PE with birth 28–34 weeks → ~1 in 3 recurrence
➔ Previous PE with birth 34–37 weeks → ~1 in 4
➔ Previous PE with birth ≥37 weeks → ~1 in 6

RCOG also highlights increased lifetime risk of hypertension, stroke and heart disease after PE.

2

HELLP syndrome

Maternal prognosis

Potentially life-threatening.

Risks: DIC, hepatic complications, renal failure, pulmonary oedema, stroke.

Usually improves after delivery.

Fetal prognosis

Mainly determined by gestational age.

High risk of FGR, placental insufficiency, prematurity and fetal death.

Future pregnancy

Increased risk of recurrent hypertensive disease/HELLP.

Requires consultant-led surveillance.

Exam pearl:

Earlier onset = worse maternal + fetal prognosis.

3

Gestational diabetes mellitus

Prognosis

Maternal

Usually resolves after delivery.

Increased future risk of:

type 2 diabetes

recurrent GDM

metabolic disease.

Fetal/neonatal

Macrosomia

Shoulder dystocia

Birth trauma

Neonatal hypoglycaemia

Respiratory morbidity

Jaundice

Stillbirth risk if poorly controlled.

Long-term offspring

Increased risk of obesity and metabolic disease.

4

Pre-existing diabetes

Generally higher-risk pregnancy than uncomplicated GDM.

Maternal

↑

pre-eclampsia

miscarriage

diabetic ketoacidosis

hypoglycaemia

progression of retinopathy/nephropathy

operative delivery.

Fetal

↑

congenital malformations

macrosomia

FGR if severe vascular disease

stillbirth

neonatal hypoglycaemia

prematurity.

Prognosis is substantially improved by excellent preconception glycaemic control and antenatal management.

5

Preterm labour

Prognosis depends predominantly on gestational age.

Gestation General prognosis
<24 weeks Very high morbidity/mortality
24–27+6 High neonatal morbidity
28–31+6 Survival substantially improves but morbidity remains significant
32–33+6 Generally good survival; respiratory/neurodevelopmental morbidity possible
34–36+6 Generally favourable but increased neonatal morbidity compared with term
Important complications

respiratory distress

IVH

NEC

sepsis

cerebral palsy

neurodevelopmental impairment.

RCOG specifically maintains guidance covering corticosteroids, PPROM, cervical cerclage and preterm birth.

SECTION 3

6. PPROM

Maternal prognosis

Chorioamnionitis

maternal sepsis

placental abruption

operative delivery.

Fetal/neonatal prognosis

Depends heavily on gestational age.

Risks:

prematurity

respiratory morbidity

neonatal sepsis

cord compression

pulmonary hypoplasia, particularly with very early prolonged oligohydramnios.

➔ Earlier PPROM → worse prognosis.
7

Placental abruption

Maternal prognosis

Potentially severe:

major haemorrhage

shock

DIC

renal failure

hysterectomy

maternal death.

Fetal prognosis

fetal hypoxia

FGR

prematurity

fetal death.

Prognosis is strongly related to extent of abruption, maternal haemorrhage and fetal status.

8

Placenta praevia

Maternal
recurrent APH

major PPH

transfusion

emergency caesarean

hysterectomy.

Fetal
prematurity

neonatal morbidity

malpresentation

fetal compromise during bleeding.

Importantly, a low-lying placenta at the 20-week scan often resolves: RCOG reports that 9/10 women with a low-lying placenta at 20 weeks no longer have it at follow-up.

9

Placenta accreta spectrum

Maternal prognosis

Potentially life-threatening:

massive obstetric haemorrhage

transfusion

bladder/ureteric injury

hysterectomy

ICU admission

maternal death.

Fetal
iatrogenic prematurity

neonatal morbidity.

Best prognosis occurs with antenatal diagnosis and planned delivery in an experienced specialist centre.

RCOG describes placenta praevia/accreta as conditions associated with substantial maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, particularly because of major haemorrhage and preterm birth.

10

Vasa praevia

Untreated/undiagnosed:

catastrophic fetal haemorrhage after membrane rupture

acute fetal hypoxia

fetal death.

Antenatally diagnosed + planned caesarean: prognosis becomes dramatically better.

Exam pearl

Antenatal diagnosis is the major prognostic determinant.

11

Multiple pregnancy

Maternal prognosis

↑

anaemia

PE

GDM

PPH

operative delivery

thromboembolism.

Fetal

Major determinant is chorionicity.

Risks:

preterm birth

FGR

TTTS

TAPS

TRAP sequence

congenital abnormalities

stillbirth.

RCOG highlights increased risks including PE, caesarean/assisted birth and PPH.
12

Fetal growth restriction

Prognosis

Depends on:

gestational age

severity

Doppler abnormalities

associated anomalies

maternal disease.

Complications:

stillbirth

hypoxia

prematurity

neonatal morbidity

neurodevelopmental impairment.

Early severe FGR + abnormal Dopplers = poor prognosis.

13

Small-for-gestational-age fetus

Distinguish from FGR.

Constitutionally small fetus

Usually good prognosis if Dopplers and growth trajectory are normal.

Pathological FGR

Higher:

stillbirth

prematurity

neonatal morbidity

long-term metabolic/cardiovascular disease.

14

Oligohydramnios

Prognosis depends on cause and gestation.

Risks:

cord compression

abnormal CTG

FGR

pulmonary hypoplasia if severe and early

musculoskeletal abnormalities with prolonged early severe oligohydramnios.

Term isolated oligohydramnios generally has a much better prognosis than early severe oligohydramnios.

15

Polyhydramnios

Mild idiopathic

Often good prognosis.

Severe/secondary

Risks:

malpresentation

preterm labour

PPROM

cord prolapse

placental abruption

PPH

fetal anomaly.

Prognosis depends largely on underlying cause.

SECTION 4

PART II — EARLY PREGNANCY

16

Miscarriage

Single early miscarriage

Usually excellent future fertility prognosis.

Most subsequent pregnancies are successful.

Recurrent miscarriage

Prognosis depends on:

maternal age

chromosomal abnormalities

uterine pathology

APS

endocrine disease

parental chromosomal rearrangement.

Important
A history of miscarriage does not automatically imply infertility.
17

Ectopic pregnancy

RCOG recognises tubal, cervical, caesarean-scar, interstitial/cornual, ovarian, abdominal and heterotopic ectopic pregnancy.

Maternal prognosis

➔ With early diagnosis and treatment → generally excellent.

Untreated ruptured ectopic:

major haemorrhage

shock

emergency surgery

maternal death.

Fertility

Depends on:

condition of contralateral tube

tubal damage

previous ectopic

underlying PID/infertility.

Recurrence

Approximately 10% after one ectopic and higher after multiple ectopics.

18

Molar pregnancy / gestational trophoblastic disease

Complete mole

Usually excellent prognosis with appropriate treatment.

Potential progression to:

persistent GTD

invasive mole

choriocarcinoma.

Choriocarcinoma

Generally highly chemotherapy-sensitive, with excellent cure rates in low-risk disease.

Poor prognosis associated with:

high FIGO score

liver/brain metastases

delayed treatment

very high hCG

antecedent term pregnancy.

SECTION 5

19. PCOS

Immediate prognosis

Chronic condition rather than a curable disease.

Fertility

Ovulatory dysfunction may cause subfertility.

Many women conceive with lifestyle intervention and/or ovulation induction.

Long-term

Increased risk of:

type 2 diabetes

metabolic syndrome

endometrial hyperplasia/cancer

cardiovascular risk factors.

RCOG has specifically addressed the long-term consequences of PCOS in its Green-top guidance.

20

Endometriosis

Prognosis

Chronic, recurrent condition.

Can cause:

chronic pelvic pain

dyspareunia

infertility/subfertility

ovarian endometrioma

recurrent symptoms after treatment.

Fertility

Severity, age, ovarian reserve and tubal factors determine prognosis.

Cancer

Small increased association with certain ovarian cancers, but malignant transformation is uncommon.

21

Fibroids

Small/asymptomatic fibroid

Usually excellent prognosis.

Submucosal/intracavitary fibroid

May cause:

infertility

miscarriage

abnormal bleeding.

Pregnancy

Depending on size/location:

pain

malpresentation

preterm birth

placental complications

PPH

caesarean delivery.

22

Adenomyosis

Prognosis

Chronic condition.

Associated with:

heavy menstrual bleeding

dysmenorrhoea

pelvic pain

infertility/subfertility.

Pregnancy may have increased risk of:

miscarriage

preterm birth

placental complications

PPH.

23

Pelvic inflammatory disease

Acute PID

Usually responds well to antibiotics if treated early.

Delayed/untreated PID

Can result in:

chronic pelvic pain

tubal infertility

ectopic pregnancy

tubo-ovarian abscess.

Fertility

Risk increases with repeated episodes.

Exam pearl:

➔ First PID episode → fertility risk; recurrent PID → substantially greater risk.
24

Ovarian cysts

Functional cyst

➔ Usually resolves spontaneously → excellent prognosis.

Benign persistent cyst

Generally excellent after appropriate management.

Torsion

Usually good if treated promptly; delayed treatment can cause ovarian necrosis.

Malignant mass

Prognosis depends heavily on stage, histology and residual disease.

25

Endometrial hyperplasia

Without atypia

Generally excellent prognosis and often regresses with progestogen therapy.

Atypical hyperplasia / EIN

Significant risk of concurrent or future endometrial carcinoma.

Exam pearl:

Atypia changes the prognosis.

RCOG specifically has Green-top guidance on management of endometrial hyperplasia.

26

Endometrial cancer

Prognostic factors

Most important:

FIGO stage

histological type

grade

myometrial invasion

LVSI

nodal/metastatic disease

molecular classification.

General pattern

➔ Stage I → generally excellent prognosis
➔ Advanced stage → progressively poorer prognosis.
27

Cervical cancer

Prognosis mainly determined by:

FIGO stage

tumour size

nodal status

parametrial involvement

distant metastases.

➔ Early-stage disease → potentially curable.
➔ Advanced/metastatic disease → substantially poorer prognosis.
28

Ovarian cancer

Prognosis depends primarily on:

FIGO stage

histological subtype

grade

residual disease after cytoreduction

molecular characteristics

response to chemotherapy.

General rule

➔ Early-stage ovarian cancer → considerably better prognosis.
➔ Advanced-stage epithelial ovarian cancer → poorer prognosis and significant recurrence risk.
29

Vulval cancer

Prognostic factors

FIGO stage

tumour size

depth of invasion

lymph-node involvement.

Node-negative disease has much better prognosis than node-positive disease.

SECTION 6

PART IV — UROGYNAECOLOGY

30

Pelvic organ prolapse

Prognosis

Not usually life-threatening.

Can be chronic/recurrent.

Symptoms:

pressure

vaginal bulge

urinary/bowel dysfunction

sexual dysfunction.

After surgery:

symptoms usually improve significantly.

recurrence remains possible.

31

Stress urinary incontinence

Prognosis

Generally good.

Pelvic floor therapy can improve symptoms.

Surgical treatment has high success but recurrence can occur.

32

Urge incontinence / overactive bladder

Usually chronic but manageable.

Prognosis:

improvement possible with bladder training, lifestyle modification and medication.

recurrence is common.

SECTION 7

PART V — GYNAECOLOGICAL EMERGENCIES

Condition Prognosis
Ovarian torsion Excellent if treated early; ovarian loss possible if delayed
Ruptured ectopic Potentially life-threatening
Tubo-ovarian abscess Usually treatable; infertility/chronic pain possible
Acute PID Good if treated early
Haemorrhagic ovarian cyst Usually self-limiting
Severe OHSS Usually resolves with treatment; thromboembolism/organ failure possible
Uterine rupture Major maternal/fetal emergency
Uterine inversion Life-threatening haemorrhage but excellent outcome with rapid treatment
Major PPH Usually good with rapid multidisciplinary management; severe cases can cause organ failure/death

RCOG identifies PPH as potentially life-threatening and stresses rapid treatment to prevent progression to major haemorrhage.

SECTION 8

PART VI — OBSTETRIC INFECTIONS

Chorioamnionitis

Maternal

Usually good with prompt antibiotics and delivery when indicated.

Untreated:

maternal sepsis

postpartum endometritis

PPH.

Neonatal

neonatal sepsis

pneumonia

meningitis

prematurity-related morbidity.

GBS colonisation

Usually excellent maternal prognosis.

Main concern is neonatal early-onset GBS disease.

➔ With appropriate intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis → neonatal risk is markedly reduced.

Maternal sepsis

Prognosis depends heavily on:

speed of recognition

source control

antimicrobial therapy

organ dysfunction.

Sepsis with organ failure = poor prognosis compared with uncomplicated infection.

SECTION 9

PART VII — COMMON OBSTETRIC PROCEDURAL OUTCOMES

Previous caesarean section

Future prognosis is generally good, but risks increase with:

number of previous CS

placenta praevia

placenta accreta spectrum

uterine rupture

adhesions

surgical complications.

VBAC

Successful VBAC:

avoids repeat abdominal surgery

generally favourable maternal outcome.

Failed TOLAC:

emergency caesarean

increased maternal/neonatal morbidity.

Uterine rupture is uncommon but potentially catastrophic.

HIGH-YIELD RCOG PROGNOSTIC RULES

⭐ Rule 1
Gestational age is one of the strongest prognostic factors

Especially for:

preterm birth

PPROM

FGR

early-onset PE

placental disease.

⭐ Rule 2
Severity determines prognosis
➔ Mild → usually favourable
➔ Severe → significantly increased morbidity/mortality
⭐ Rule 3
Antenatal diagnosis improves prognosis

Especially:

placenta accreta

vasa praevia

fetal anomalies

FGR

multiple pregnancy.

⭐ Rule 4
Early treatment improves prognosis

Especially:

ectopic pregnancy

PPH

sepsis

ovarian torsion

PID.

⭐ Rule 5
Fetal prognosis is often determined by prematurity

For PE, PPROM, abruption, FGR and placenta praevia.

⭐ Rule 6
Recurrence matters

Particularly:

PE

GDM

miscarriage

ectopic pregnancy

preterm birth

placental abruption

PPH

endometriosis.

⭐ Rule 7
Cancer prognosis = stage first

For:

cervical

endometrial

ovarian

vulval cancers.

EXAM HIGH-YIELD DRILL

High-Yield Drill

Core associations, key diagnostic parameters, and rapid exam differentiation:

📌 PART I — OBSTETRIC CONDITIONS
  • ● Chronic hypertension: Usually good if controlled; increased risk of superimposed PE, abruption, stroke
  • ● Gestational hypertension: Usually favourable, but may progress to PE
  • ● Mild pre-eclampsia: Generally favourable with surveillance
  • ● Early-onset PE: Higher maternal morbidity
  • ● Severe PE: Potentially life-threatening: stroke, HELLP, renal/liver complications
  • ● Eclampsia: Usually recover with treatment, but risk of stroke, aspiration, organ failure and death
📌 PART V — GYNAECOLOGICAL EMERGENCIES
  • ● Ovarian torsion: Excellent if treated early; ovarian loss possible if delayed
  • ● Ruptured ectopic: Potentially life-threatening
  • ● Tubo-ovarian abscess: Usually treatable; infertility/chronic pain possible
  • ● Acute PID: Good if treated early
  • ● Haemorrhagic ovarian cyst: Usually self-limiting
  • ● Severe OHSS: Usually resolves with treatment; thromboembolism/organ failure possible
