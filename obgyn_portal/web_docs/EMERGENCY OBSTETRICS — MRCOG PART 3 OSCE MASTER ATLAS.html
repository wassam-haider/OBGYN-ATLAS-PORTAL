SECTION 1

Overview & Core Guidance

➔ Recognise → call for help → ABCDE → diagnose while resuscitating → quantify severity → investigate → definitive treatment → maternal/fetal monitoring → MDT → communicate → document/debrief.

I have aligned this with current RCOG/NICE guidance where available. Important update: RCOG's placenta praevia/PAS guideline was updated in June 2026, while several emergency guidelines remain older and are under review. RCOG currently lists APH, PPH, maternal collapse, VTE, cord prolapse, shoulder dystocia and sepsis among its relevant Green-top guidance.

SECTION 4

3. PLACENTA PRAEVIA

Definition

Placenta located in the lower uterine segment such that it approaches or covers the internal cervical os.

Classic OSCE presentation

Painless, bright-red vaginal bleeding in the second half of pregnancy.

Typical examination

uterus:

soft

non-tender

fetus may be:

malpresenting

high/mobile

fetal heart may initially be normal.

Important distinction
Feature Praevia Abruption
Bleeding Bright red Darker may occur
Pain Usually painless Painful
Uterus Soft Tender/tense
Contractions Usually absent Often present
FHR Often initially normal Often abnormal
Shock Can be severe May be disproportionate
Visible bleeding Usually corresponds better to loss Concealed bleeding possible

Diagnosis

Ultrasound

transvaginal ultrasound is safe and more accurate for placental localisation.

Assess:

placental edge

relationship to internal os

previous CS scar

signs of PAS.

The current RCOG guideline emphasises early identification of placenta praevia and placenta accreta spectrum because the combination carries major haemorrhage risk.

MRCOG management

Acute bleed

ABCDE

➔ → senior help
➔ → IV access
➔ → bloods/crossmatch
➔ → CTG
➔ → ultrasound
➔ → assess haemodynamic status.

If unstable:

resuscitate

activate major obstetric haemorrhage pathway

prepare urgent birth.

If stable:

assess gestation

bleeding severity

fetal condition

recurrence

placental location

PAS risk.

Delivery

If placenta praevia persists and covers the os:

vaginal birth is generally contraindicated.

caesarean birth planned according to clinical circumstances and guideline recommendations.

Never say “all placenta praevia = elective CS at exactly X weeks” without considering current RCOG guidance, bleeding history, PAS and local protocol.

SECTION 5

4. PLACENTAL ABRUPTION

Definition

Premature separation of a normally situated placenta before delivery.

Classic presentation

Painful vaginal bleeding + abdominal pain + tender/tense uterus + fetal compromise.

But remember

Bleeding may be concealed.

Therefore:

Do not underestimate abruption because visible vaginal blood loss is small.

Clinical types

Revealed

Blood escapes through vagina.

Concealed

Blood accumulates behind placenta.

Mixed

Both.

Typical findings

abdominal pain

back pain

uterine tenderness

hypertonus

frequent contractions

fetal distress

absent FHR if severe

maternal shock

DIC

Severe abruption

May cause:

➔ Abruption → tissue factor release → coagulation activation → consumption of fibrinogen/platelets → DIC.

Laboratory clue

Low fibrinogen is particularly concerning.

Management
Stable mother + reassuring fetus

Depends on:

gestational age

amount of bleeding

ongoing bleeding

fetal status

maternal condition.

Unstable mother

Maternal resuscitation takes priority.

Fetal compromise + vaginal birth not imminent

➔ → urgent birth, usually caesarean unless vaginal birth is safer/imminent.

IUFD

If mother stable:

vaginal birth generally preferred.

If maternal instability/coagulopathy:

expedite delivery and correct coagulopathy.

Diagnostic framework

After 20 weeks:

BP ≥140/90 mmHg

PLUS:

maternal organ dysfunction or

Proteinuria

or ACR ≥8 mg/mmol

Severe features

➔ Loss of reflexes → respiratory depression → respiratory arrest → cardiac arrest

Important red flags

Oliguria

SECTION 25

24. MATERNAL CARDIAC ARREST — PREGNANCY

Key modifications
Call cardiac arrest team.

CPR immediately.

Manual left uterine displacement if uterus is sufficiently large to cause aortocaval compression.

Defibrillation uses standard adult energy.

Do not withhold defibrillation because of pregnancy.

Identify reversible causes.

Prepare for resuscitative hysterotomy if indicated and ROSC is not achieved.

Timing principle

If cardiac arrest occurs at/above approximately 20 weeks, prepare for resuscitative hysterotomy immediately; aim to commence by 4 minutes and deliver by 5 minutes where indicated.

SECTION 26

25. CORD PROLAPSE

RCOG defines cord prolapse as descent of the cord alongside or past the presenting part after membrane rupture. Incidence is approximately 0.1–0.6%, and is higher with breech presentation.

Risk factors

polyhydramnios

SECTION 31

30. SHOULDER DYSTOCIA — OSCE ALGORITHM

Immediately

CALL FOR HELP

senior obstetrician

midwife coordinator

anaesthetist

neonatal team.

Stop

Do not use excessive downward traction.

Ask mother to stop pushing.

FIRST-LINE

McROBERTS manoeuvre

flatten bed

hyperflex maternal hips

thighs onto abdomen.

downward

SECTION 41

40. UNIVERSAL MRCOG EMERGENCY CLOSING STATEMENT

For almost any emergency station, finish with:

The MRCOG mindset

Do not get trapped trying to make the diagnosis before treating the patient.

The safest sequence is:

➔ RECOGNISE → CALL → RESUSCITATE → INVESTIGATE → DIAGNOSE → DEFINITIVE TREATMENT → REASSESS → ESCALATE → COMMUNICATE → DOCUMENT.
